Register Spill and Joy & Curiosity

A weekly essay written against a timer, a links section bolted onto the bottom, and the series that section became.
SourceRegister Spill, About page (Substack) ↗
TL;DR — Register Spill is Thorsten Ball's Substack newsletter, started in April 2023 as one short essay a week, written in a timed sitting and published as is. In August 2024 he added a links section called Joy & Curiosity to the bottom of five posts; from September 2024 the section became the whole issue, numbered from #6. By 21 November 2026 there are 102 standalone issues (#6 to #107). Each one is a personal opening followed by a long list of links with his comments. Over two years the opening grew from a short anecdote into a working argument about what coding agents do to software, and AI went from a side topic to the subject of every issue.

If you want to know what Thorsten is thinking about this week, Joy & Curiosity is the place to look. The craft essays on the next page come out a few times a year now; this comes out on most Sundays.

The name and the timer

The first post, What is Register Spill? (2 April 2023), sets the rules. He wanted to publish more writing that is longer than a thread and shorter than a blog post, and he limited the effort so that he would actually keep going:

The idea is that once a week I set myself a timer for 60min and what I write in that time gets published.— Thorsten Ball, What is Register Spill?

The name is a compiler joke, and it fits someone who wrote two books on interpreters and compilers. A register allocator has only a few CPU registers to work with. When it runs out, it "spills" values to the stack in memory. The newsletter is where he spills the thoughts that no longer fit in his head. He credits someone named Patrick with suggesting it. His stated goal was ten posts, because he was not sure he could keep it up.

The About page describes the same format with one detail added: the timer was set every Sunday, and he published when it rang. The day stuck. Of the 102 Joy & Curiosity issues in the archive, 69 are dated Sunday, 31 Saturday and 2 Friday (from the dates in the post metadata).

Timeboxing the writing makes it a habit rather than a project. A post that has to be finished in an hour cannot be a polished essay, so it ends up as a clear statement of one idea plus an example. That is why the 2023 and early-2024 posts read as quick notes on a single engineering point. The next page covers those.

How Joy & Curiosity started

The section first appears at the bottom of Things you can do with codebases on 4 August 2024, under the plain heading "Joy & Curiosity". He presents it as a return to the newsletter's original idea, telling readers what is on his mind that week:

In this section I want to share things from the previous week I find interesting.— Thorsten Ball, Things you can do with codebases

The same section, with the same introduction, runs under four more August essays: It's in the stories (10 Aug), Second's an Eternity (17 Aug), No, really: YAGNI (25 Aug) and Leave something for tomorrow (31 Aug). None of the five sections has a number. The next week came Joy & Curiosity #6 (7 September 2024), the first issue with the name as its title. It opens by saying the issue "consists of only the Joy & Curiosity section". He gave two reasons: he wanted writing time back for side projects, and he thought the essays might improve if they were not on a weekly schedule.

So the count works out like this: five unnumbered sections in August, then an issue numbered six. Treating those five sections as #1–#5 is the only reading that makes the numbering add up. He never says so in the posts I checked, so take it as an inference. One more clue fits it. #9 explains that the two posts in between (Skin-Shedding Code and Glad I did it in Go) had no Joy & Curiosity section, and neither of them took a number. The standalone issues then run from #6 to #107 without a gap.

Note the URL trap. #6 lives at the slug joy-and-curiosity-70, and #38 at joy-and-curiosity-37-f50. Every link on this page comes from the archive metadata, not from the issue number.

Anatomy of an issue

Every issue carries the same Substack subtitle, "Interesting & joyful things from the previous week". Each one has the same four parts:

  • The opening. Anything from two lines to a short essay: where he is writing from (an airport, a hotel bar, a train back from a meetup), what happened that week, or a question he is chewing on. Some weeks he says up front that there is no intro.
  • A hand-drawn rule. All 102 issues contain at least one wide, thin image, and #52 confirms these lines are drawn by hand as a signature of "an original J&C". The median issue has two.
  • The list. Bullets, each a link and his commentary on it, which usually ends with a quoted line from the source and a sentence on why it stuck with him. The commentary is the point: he says what he thought while reading, so the list reads as one person's week rather than a feed.
  • The sign-off. 95 of 102 issues end with a subscribe nudge, usually a joke riffing on something in that issue. #78, which opened with an oil-industry analogy, ends by asking whether you also like to deem yourself an oil industry expert in your newsletter.

The list barely changed shape over two years. It got longer. What changed is the top of the issue: by 2026 the median opening is more than three times the 2024 length, and in many weeks it is effectively the week's essay. The weekly essay that #6 gave up came back, moved to the top of the link issue.

In 2025 and 2026 combined, he published six posts outside the series: Judging Code, How might AI change programming?, There's Beauty in AI, A New Kind of Code, Building Software Is Learning and Ownership. Those few essays draw far more reactions than any link issue (35 to 91, against a series high of 40). Readers save their likes for the essays, and the link issue is the weekly habit.

The cadence is close to weekly but not strict: most months have four issues, and there are visible breaks. The longest was a planned summer break in 2026. #89 (7 June) announced it after "three years of writing this newsletter on my weekends", and #90 (4 July) came back saying he had fallen into a rut following the recipe and would "double down on having fun with it".

Three things stand out. First, AI went from a third of the 2024 issues to every single 2026 issue, and "agent" went from zero to 39 of 40. An early sign is #21 (January 2025), where he tells readers to set a 45-minute timer and build something with aider and Claude. Amp first appears in #34 (5 April 2025), announcing the ampcode.com waitlist and the first podcast episodes. Second, books never went away: two thirds of the issues mention them, and The Power Broker alone comes up in 12 issues, as he reports slow progress through it and then finishes it in #43. Third, "craft" as a word is rare, even though craft is the topic of much of his writing. He shows it through the links and the commentary rather than naming it.

The most-linked places, by number of issues: x.com (94 issues, nearly all of them), YouTube (64), GitHub (54), Wikipedia (41), ampcode.com (39), Hacker News (27) and The New Yorker (26). Among individual writers' sites, Simon Willison's blog leads with 15 issues, followed by Armin Ronacher's lucumr.pocoo.org, Sean Goedecke's seangoedecke.com and Experimental History at 13 each, then Mitchell Hashimoto's and antirez's blogs at 8 each. Most of those are engineers writing in public about what they actually do with these tools.

How the tone shifted, 2024 to 2026

2024: a personal week in links. The early openings are short and domestic. #12 is about a new mattress. #13 is about rewatching Jaws and asking which software feels like it came from a single person's vision. #16 is written from a hotel bar. The links range widely: Apple's in-box iPhone updaters, Palantir, Nabokov's opinions on other writers, Quake 3 networking. The tone matches the subtitle: things that were interesting and joyful.

2025: the job takes over the week. He left Zed and went back to Sourcegraph in January, Amp launched in the spring, and the openings start to report from inside the work. In #31 he says he has been writing code "like a maniac" all week. #58 is short because Amp Free launched that Wednesday. Family and travel stay in the openings (a gravel bike, packing for trips, a back spasm), which is why the kids/family count peaks in 2025. But the list fills up with posts on how people use models to write code.

2026: the opening becomes an argument. From January the opening is often a sustained case that most engineers have not grasped how much cheap code changes. #72 asks why some programmers still say models cannot code. #75 asks where software goes when an assistant can build the app you wanted on the spot. #78 is the rainwater-to-oil analogy. #80 offers a theory of when agents succeed. #81 argues that agents write unconfident code. He also hedges: #83 warns readers that he, like everyone, has been making predictions, and that second-order effects are hard to call. The voice stays playful, with typed-out exclamations and running jokes, but the subject has narrowed. The summer reset in #90 is the one time he says out loud that the recipe had become a rut.

What I learned over the course of the year is that typing out code by hand now frustrates me.— Thorsten Ball, Joy & Curiosity #68

That line from the first issue of 2026 is the hinge. He writes that for more than fifteen years he thought he loved typing code. He concludes the pleasure was never the typing. It was learning, making computers do new things, and sharing them, and all of that survives agents.

The second part is five issues picked for the opening itself, because each marks a turn in his story or his thinking:

  • #24 (2025-01-26): his last day at Zed. He sums up a full year there and announces he is returning to Sourcegraph, the company he left at the end of 2023.
  • #65 (2025-12-07): Amp becomes its own company, Amp, Inc., and he is one of twenty cofounders. He says he could not have imagined this when he left Zed a year earlier.
  • #68 (2026-01-03): after more than fifteen years, he asks whether he ever loved typing code. He decides that what he loves is learning, building and sharing, which agents leave intact.
  • #75 (2026-02-21): where does software go when you can ask an assistant for a workout plan and get an app, or skip the app altogether? His own example is a family shopping list that may not need Todoist.
  • #95 (2026-08-16): two weeks of shipped features, a hidden game and twenty bug fixes, all done remotely through Amp in orbs (remote machines for agents) without touching a local development environment.
Code is no longer hard-to-find, only-in-some-places, hard-to-extract.— Thorsten Ball, Joy & Curiosity #78

How to read it

To catch up, read the openings rather than the archive in order. The opening is where his own view is, and the links show what fed it that week. The About page lists his own picks of classic Register Spill posts; nearly all are standalone essays from 2023 and 2024, which the next page covers. The agent ideas that keep surfacing in the 2026 openings are worked out at length in his Amp notes and talks, covered later on this map.
