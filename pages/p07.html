Raising an Agent, Season 2: orbs, jellyware and the company of the future

Eight conversations between Quinn Slack and Thorsten Ball, from “the local dev env is dead” in July to “features are meaningless now” in November.
SourceRaising an Agent on ampcode.com ↗
TL;DR — Season 1 was about the coding agent. Season 2 is about what comes after it. Amp rebuilt itself around remote agents that run in disposable cloud machines called orbs, and the hosts spend eight episodes working out what that does to everything around the code. Their answers: local development stops mattering, the agent should prove its work and then ship, watch and fix it in production, mandatory code review slows things down without making them safer, and a small team with no departments, RFCs or quarterly goals can, they claim, match competitors with thirty times the staff. The season ends on the hardest version of the claim, that when features can be dictated into existence a feature list is no longer a reason to buy software.

The episodes are long, loose and full of shop talk about Amp. This page reads them as one argument and pins each step of it to the minute where it is made.

What changed between seasons

Season 1 ran ten episodes from March 2025 to February 2026 (dates from the podcast feed). Then the Amp team went heads-down. On 19 February 2026 they published The Coding Agent Is Dead, which said the agent in the editor sidebar was finished and killed Amp's VS Code and Cursor extensions. On 6 May came a rebuilt CLI, codename Neo, on a new architecture that can be remote-controlled from the web and manages its own context. On 30 June came Agents in Orbs. Season 2 opened on 17 July, less than three weeks after orbs shipped, and the hosts had been living in them since.

The vocabulary is Amp's own, and it piles up fast. Here is each term once, defined only as far as a written source or a clear passage in an episode supports.

S2E1 · The Local Dev Env is Dead

Released 17 Jul 2026 · 48:31 · episode page · YouTube

The opener compares the moment to 2025, when agents first got to spend tokens freely: this time the shift is from one agent on a laptop to many agents in isolated machines. The evidence is mostly small. A screenshot of a misbehaving tooltip goes into a new orb thread, and after a customer call the fix is waiting with its own verification, for about the effort of filing a ticket. The hosts say most competitors treat cloud agents as event-driven helpers for pull requests, while Amp treats them as the next generation of the product. With a clean, standardised machine per agent, re-running CI on a feature branch starts to look redundant. They concede limits: some credentials, production database access above all, could not yet go safely into an orb.

S2E2 · Orbs and Jellyware

Released 11 Aug 2026 · 55:17 · episode page · YouTube

Recorded in person in Munich during a team week. Several engineers had resisted orbs, and within about four weeks everyone had switched without being told to; when the team's laptops were wiped, people did not bother restoring their dotfiles. The explanation they give is friction: even a 1% cost to starting a parallel checkout suppresses work, a point Thorsten also makes in his note What I Want to Tell You About Orbs. An agent that ties up no laptop can be asked for exhaustive proof (a test matrix, a demo video, frame-by-frame checks of an animation), which Thorsten links to his 2024 post The Basics and its rule to test manually and record it. The episode then names jellyware and draws a conclusion about bugs: when a fix takes minutes, a team can take more risk, and a zero-bug target just means shipping too slowly. The hedges: Quinn still opens diffs to look for red flags, and Thorsten spent two months early in the year going over the core of the codebase up close so that agents had a solid base to build on.

S2E3 · When Tokens Flow Like Electricity

Released 27 Aug 2026 · 48:02 · episode page · YouTube

Thorsten opens with predictions: intelligence keeps getting cheaper and faster, and counting tokens will soon look like counting watts.

It's like electricity. You're not measuring the watts coming out of your wall.— Thorsten Ball, Raising an Agent S2E3, 03:47

Most of the episode is build versus buy, and Quinn presses on an apparent contradiction: Amp says orbs cannot be replaced by EC2, a VPN and tmux, yet Thorsten one-shotted a bug tracker in place of Linear. Thorsten's answer is that issue workflows differ in every company, while the orb product rests on design decisions above the code, such as a fresh orb for every thread. Quinn's answer is about where time goes: once the agent is where you work, the tool it displaced is low-value and you only need the 10% of it you still use. Both hedge in the same place, since a one-off tool needs a solid core under it, and the episode ends on paying for software made by someone whose judgment you trust.

S2E4 · What Is the Computer For Now?

Released 11 Sep 2026 · 53:18 · episode page · YouTube

Here a bug fix no longer ends at push: the agent watches the deploy, emails the user who reported the bug once the fix is live, searches the logs for others who hit it, and keeps watching the feature. Thorsten describes cheap evals, in which one orb spawns others with tasks that touch a new production-testing tool without mentioning it, then checks whether they used it. Quinn addresses the recent outages; Thorsten had sorted the 18 to 20 incidents of the previous six weeks into upstream providers, scaling infrastructure and shipped broken code, and the hosts put push-to-main at about a third by count and far less by downtime (their figures). Their stance is to go as fast as possible right up to the point where things break, accepting that calibrating sometimes means crossing it. The last third asks what a personal computer is still for.

I'm no longer just skeptical of local development. I'm now wondering, what is the role of the computer as a personal device in the future?— Thorsten Ball, Raising an Agent S2E4, 40:18

S2E5 · Stop Boxing In Your Agent

Released 25 Sep 2026 · 56:27 · episode page · YouTube

A late-night “Hot Take Thursday” answering What I believe about the future of software development, a list first posted on X and put on his blog on 19 September. The episode opens with a relative's consultancy that capped engineers at €50 of tokens a week and then moved them to a cheaper model; Thorsten's objection is arithmetic, because if output is wrong one time in ten, someone has to find which time. He repeats the post's line that some people will be priced out of frontier software development, as a prediction he dislikes, and compares it to the cost of home computers in the 1980s. The title comes from the middle: agents went vertical through the stack first, the next step is horizontal across the whole lifecycle, and approval chains, scattered repos and mandatory review are the walls in the way. He explains runners as a bridge for hardware, GPUs and Macs, and closes with the hot take he left out of the post: that so many engineers turned out conservative, as if they had never read how Java or JavaScript were first received.

Don't wait for your agent. You're wasting time. Let them wait for you.— Thorsten Ball, Raising an Agent S2E5, 31:43

S2E6 · The Company of the Future

Released 20 Oct 2026 · 1:10:50 · episode page · YouTube

Recorded together in San Francisco, it answers the question they get asked most, how the team works; Thorsten later called it possibly the most important episode of the year (Joy & Curiosity #103). The setup is 21 people with no departments, executives or territory, and one scheduled meeting a week. Quality control works through visibility: a work-in-progress channel, feature flags turned on for the team first, two blunt feedback channels, and news posts written before the feature is built. Review goes through the agent (ask for proof, read the files it touched, look closely only at hot paths, tag a colleague into the thread), and there are no quarterly goals because a six-month plan does not survive the model releases. The “Sunday post” in the episode description is a message Thorsten sends to the team and is not public; his written account of the week is Joy & Curiosity #106, which adds the meeting's four sections (Leave, Talk, Shipping, News Posts) and the two-to-three-minute timer for silent writing.

S2E7 · Culture Stops Slop

Released 30 Oct 2026 · 1:00:26 · episode page · YouTube

This one answers the reactions to S2E6 (Thorsten's summary is in Joy & Curiosity #104). Some listeners stopped doing PRs; others asked Amp to meet them where they are, which Thorsten says in 2026 means building something useless within six months. Two cases show architecture without RFCs: self-hosted orbs were reshaped on site with a customer whose security team refused inbound connections, settled in one Slack thread and documented through a news post that served as the design document, and macOS Satellites came out of a conversation at one table after Morbs proved slow and expensive. On slop, both hosts put the cause in incentives and ownership, and say a review gate fixes neither. Thorsten says he no longer reads unit tests for product code and that undoing an architectural wrong turn is now cheap, with a hedge for production data migrations and the story of one of his own column removals that caused errors during a rollout.

S2E8 · Features Are Meaningless Now

Released 18 Nov 2026 · 1:10:34 · episode page · YouTube

Two launches frame it, Spaces and Boxes. Asked what Spaces are for, the hosts give different answers (one wants focus, the other a coordinator so he stops relaying context between threads), and they defend shipping before they had worked it out because a model release can move the product's centre of gravity. From there comes the title: if anyone can add a feature quickly, a feature list stops being a reason to choose a product, and the competitor that matters is a customer who builds the 2% they need. Boxes extend jellyware to running software, a cheap computer whose agent edits the live database for you so that no admin screen is needed. The episode ends on a limit: several new features had conflicting permission rules, no agent would have stopped that, and the fix is a specification and coordination job they handed to one person to own end to end.

The big ideas of season 2

The local dev env is dead; parallel remote agents replace it

The claim is narrower than it sounds. Nobody says local machines are useless. The point is that a local checkout carries just enough friction (ports, processes, dirty worktrees, a busy CPU) to stop you starting the tenth agent, and that once the friction reaches zero, people work differently (S2E2, 11:01). A second effect follows: local setups that nobody maintains decay quickly, so the switch locks itself in (S2E4, 12:49). The hedges are real. Some credentials could not go into orbs yet (S2E1, 09:09). Hardware and Apple platforms needed runners and later Satellites, which Thorsten calls a bridge that complements orbs (S2E5, 43:21).

Jellyware: high quality, changed by asking

Jellyware sits between hand-crafted software and vibe coding. The bar for quality stays high, but you change the thing by talking to an agent. Thorsten's example is a school-festival shop app that came with an admin interface he would never use, because sending a new photo of the menu was faster (S2E2, 40:13). He did the same with a browser router he built for $5 in tokens (Joy & Curiosity #99). The idea grows over the season. In S2E3 it describes how the team itself uses Amp (the two Amps, 33:17). In S2E8 Boxes are pitched as the way to give customers that same power over their own tools (48:26, and Joy & Curiosity #107).

Tokens as electricity, and build versus buy

If intelligence keeps getting cheaper, rationing tokens is a sign you are in the wrong job (S2E3, 05:21). The same premise drives the hard line in S2E7: you are judged on output, and complaining that others have more tokens is like complaining in 2003 that a rival had better internet (59:27). Cheap tokens also tip build versus buy, though the line keeps moving because each model release shifts the weights (S2E3, 17:36). Their resolution is to build the thin custom layer and buy the solid core: auth, payments and whatever is load-bearing and costs money to run (31:12).

Agents that ship, monitor and fix production

Season 1 celebrated agents that run the compiler and read the errors. Season 2 makes production the feedback loop (S2E2, 21:30). The agent gets time-limited, read-only access to logs and a copy of part of the database. The hosts argue this is safer than a developer laptop with standing production access (S2E6, 47:28). Small Amp plugins let it look up a user, grant a feature flag or draft an email that a human approves (51:36). The incident review in S2E4 is the counterweight. Speed has a price, and making the system legible to an agent (quieter logs, the same graphs humans see) is still work in progress (33:56).

No mandatory code review: proof and culture instead

Thorsten's September list stated the position flatly:

Code review will die. I mean: it’s already dead.— Thorsten Ball, What I believe about the future of software development

Two things replace it in the episodes. The first is proof: an agent with a machine to itself can show videos, screenshots and a test matrix, and a reviewer who starts from that reads the diff differently (S2E6, 36:04). The second is trunk-based development with small changes behind flags. Quinn argues that a queue of unmerged branches is the actual source of risk (50:03). What keeps slop off main is culture: people own what they ship, talk to the customers who use it, and get called out in a channel everyone reads (S2E7, 31:18). Quinn adds the economist's point that review's costs go unseen while the bugs it catches are visible (57:52). The concession is there too: Quinn still opens diffs to look for red flags (S2E2, 30:54).

How a team of 21 runs without RFCs or quarterly goals

The hosts tie everything to the team's size. With no plans to hire, problems that a growing company would solve with headcount have to be solved with focus. Quinn compares it to a container ship, where you throw the least important cargo overboard first (S2E7, 38:34). Agents make each person reach further, so the old team boundaries cause collisions. That is Quinn's image of fish in a pond after someone adds fertiliser (S2E6, 58:47). Their answer is to drop territory and record the intent in agent threads, so anyone can pick up anyone's work. They admit this shapes the product too. Amp spun out of Sourcegraph partly to get this structure (60:19), and they say they do not know how a 300-person company makes the transition (65:54). They also note that agent work can turn into busy-work juggling that crowds out the deep project (S2E7, 37:30).

Features are meaningless now

This is the season's endpoint, and it was set up in the first episode: when customers can change code, code becomes the most flexible settings screen there is (S2E1, 33:59). By S2E8 the claim covers all software. A feature matrix no longer separates you from competitors, because anyone can build the features. What separates you is the way of working you sell and the judgment of the people behind it (16:22). Quinn takes it furthest by asking whether Amp could drop its settings screen and let Puck handle configuration. Thorsten is visibly uneasy, because seeing the options is how you find out what exists (68:21). The permissions mess from the same episode is the limit: specifying what the system should do is still human work.
