How Thorsten works

The habits that show up again and again in his writing and talks, from a Sunday timer in 2023 to prompting a coding agent on stage in 2026.
SourceThink Harder: How I Prompt (Laracon US, July 2026) ↗
TL;DR — Thorsten works to short feedback loops and writes for the reader. He started his newsletter by writing against a Sunday timer. He treats a slow shell or a slow test as a bug. He plans before he types and stops for the day only when he knows the next step. He has grown more sceptical of test suites over the years and more insistent on manual proof. He prompts agents the way he would brief a senior engineer, and the team he helps run expects one person to own a change from the problem to production.

The HTML of his homepage still holds a list headed “What I value”, commented out so that visitors no longer see it. Two of its lines make a good key to this page: “Simplicity, minimalism, rawness, honesty, essence” and “Discipline, craft, professionalism”. The habits below are those words in practice.

Write to a deadline, for a reader

Register Spill began as a test of whether he could write every week. His about page says he set a timer each Sunday, wrote what was on his mind, and published when the timer rang. The name comes from the same idea: what is in his registers that week. The page describes this in the past tense. Most issues are now Joy & Curiosity, a weekly note plus a long list of links, so treat the timer as how the habit started rather than a rule he still follows.

The timer caps the cost of a post: it always gets finished, polished or not. The same Sunday slot shows up inside Amp. In Raising an Agent S2E6 he says that on Sundays he thinks about the most important things for the coming week, and that over the past year he has sent the team around 25 Sunday posts on priorities and on how they build software.

He has argued for a long time that writing is real work and that friction in producing it matters. His 2020 post No, typing can be the bottleneck answers the old line that typing speed is irrelevant to programming. Engineers type a lot besides code, and he says effort is what counts, not words per minute: if writing feels like a chore, you skip it. Six years later his Laracon talk makes the same point about prompts: the goal of all writing is to be understood, and a prompt belongs in the same category as a commit message or a Slack message. You ask what the reader knows and what they are missing. His git post applies the rule to commits: the message should carry the why, because the diff already shows the what, and who you are writing for should decide how much you write (How I use git).

Treat waiting as a defect

The post that names this habit is Allergic to Waiting (July 2023). Its core story is a pairing session where a colleague expected a file-generation command to take ten minutes. Thorsten knew it took 15 to 30 seconds on his machine, insisted they look, and found a broken shell setup that spawned shells recursively. What bothers him is the shrug. His theory is that many programmers have no sense of what a computer can do in a second, so they cannot tell a slow task from a broken one.

He applies this to the smallest loops first. How fast is your shell? (January 2024) measures his zsh startup at about 70 ms and treats anything past 200 or 300 ms as hidden weight. The argument is about frequency: you start shells and render prompts so often that 10 ms per plugin adds up. His fixes are to profile first, then run fewer commands, keep the prompt simple and do less.

Those who don’t honor the milliseconds will end up with seconds.— Thorsten Ball, How fast is your shell?

The same instinct runs through his git habits. Aliases save keystrokes he does not want to waste. He opens a draft PR with the first commit so CI starts working while he does (How I use git). At product scale it becomes From 1s to 4ms, a Zed speed-up covered on page 03.

Own your setup, and keep trying new tools

His setup posts are detailed because he treats the setup as part of the craft. When he started at Zed in January 2024 he documented setting up a new MacBook from scratch rather than restoring the system from a backup, so that no cruft carries over: Homebrew, his dotfiles, a Brewfile, and a short list of files that do get copied (New year, new job, new machine). One of those is his shell history. He calls ~/.zsh_history “me”, and a follow-up post explains why: on one machine it held 26,278 commands going back to April 2019, and fuzzy search over it replaces scripts he would never have known to write in advance (Which command did you run 1731 days ago?). The habit is to keep a record cheaply now and make it searchable, instead of deciding up front what will matter.

He is attached to his tools but will drop them. After around twenty years of Vim he switched to Zed in December 2023. He says in From Vim to Zed that he tries new tools on a regular basis because there is often something worth learning or carrying back into an old workflow. He cites language-server autocomplete and inlay hints, and dropping tmux to see what Ghostty felt like without it. His April 2024 desk tour gives his hardware the same attention.

Agents loosened this. By May 2025, in the Amp note on how he used Amp, he was working mostly in VS Code with the agent in the sidebar and said the choice of editor no longer felt important. That note is now archived and marked as out of date, so read it as a snapshot of mid-2025 rather than his current setup. The care moved from the editor to the context he gives the agent, covered below.

Plan first, then keep momentum

Making a plan (June 2023) is his case for writing a task breakdown before writing code. His example is an avatar-upload feature in a Rails app: a list of tasks from the database migration up to the frontend, with open questions at the bottom. He admits the plan is incomplete and will change. Its value is the thinking it forces, because writing it out is how you discover on day one, not day two, that you have no answer for what happens to an old avatar. His method: write tasks in a todo.md, re-read until a pass changes nothing, show a colleague, then estimate and order.

The good news: making plans helps you get better at making plans.— Thorsten Ball, Making a plan

The plan pairs with momentum. In Momentum (September 2023) he argues you can create it on purpose: break work into small tasks, and start a session with an easy one, such as tidying the README or adding a test, so that the hard task comes after three finished commits. Leave something for tomorrow (August 2024) covers the other end of the day. He stops mid-task, but only when he knows exactly what comes next, so the next morning starts with typing instead of deciding. He credits the trick to Zach Holman and notes Hemingway gave the same advice for prose.

His git habits follow the same pattern: commits as quicksaves, PRs small and merged quickly, and a week-old PR treated as a warning sign (How I use git). By 2026 the planning has moved into the agent thread. In S2E6 he says he has spent no time in recent months writing design documents. When he felt stuck on a feature, he described to the model what he wanted users to see when it shipped, and it came back with a plan to split the work into a sequence he could ship piece by piece.

Tests for feedback, proof for confidence

His views on testing have shifted more than any other habit on this page, and he has written about each stage. In May 2023 tests were his REPL. In What do others do without tests? he describes them as code he can run with a couple of keystrokes from the editor, a way to narrow down a bug, and a suite that runs in under a second as a pleasure in itself. Correctness came second. In August 2023 he added a design claim: a system that is easy to test is usually easy to extend and debug, though the reverse does not hold (Programming in a Well-Tested System).

By March 2024 the doubts had grown. A Few Words on Testing opens with his credentials (more than half the code he has written is tests) and then lists what wore him down: flaky tests, slow integration suites that turned out to test nothing, and hours spent proving a fix he had already verified by hand. He then notes that Zed and Ghostty, two of the best codebases he knows, have fewer tests than he expected, concentrated on core data structures and fast to run.

Maybe the tests are only a symptom. A symptom of something else that causes the quality.— Thorsten Ball, A Few Words on Testing

He ends that post on Kent Beck’s line about testing as little as possible to reach a given level of confidence, and on people who care. The 2026 version puts more weight on manual proof. His Ownership note says that in nearly every case you can confirm a change works yourself, by running it, poking at the data, taking screenshots or recording a demo. In S2E6 he describes reviewing agent work by first asking the agent for “irrefutable proof” that it works end to end, or a matrix of what it tested, and only then reading the code.

Prompt the way you would brief a senior engineer

His Laracon US talk, Think Harder: How I Prompt (29 July 2026), says early that there is no secret technique. He uses no MCP servers, one or two skills and no custom commands. The talk rests on one question he wants you to ask before sending anything: how is the model supposed to know what I mean? The answer can only come from the training data or the context window, which holds the system prompt, tools, conversation, tool results and the prompt itself. He illustrates it with a senior engineer snatched off the street, hood pulled off in a room with a laptop and a note that says “fix the bug with the upload”. That is the model’s position. Unlike a person, it will not ask which bug. It will guess and say you are right.

The prompts he shows follow from that question. The big one, for a feature that runs Amp headless on other machines under remote control, points at three folders and three files in its first two paragraphs, then spells out the feature and its constraints, such as no checkouts and no worktrees. A shorter pattern he calls the one-two punch: first ask the agent to find the relevant code, then make the request once that code is in context. When he wanted the web UI’s message queue fixed, he first pointed it at the CLI as the reference for correct behaviour. He says about 90% of his prompts include screenshots, often of a Slack thread where a user reported the problem, because the screenshot carries the context he would otherwise have to type.

The step he likes best is the last one. His favourite prompt asks for a UI fix plus a screenshot of the Storybook story, and the agent manages it because AGENTS.md files in the repo explain how to start the dev server and where Storybook runs. Once the codebase holds the how-to, the prompt can shrink to “take a screenshot”. In the Q&A he explains how he keeps a mental map of code he did not type. You learn which parts are load-bearing, such as the protocol between components and the business invariants. You write those constraints down, have the agent check against them, and watch changes to them closely. CSS at the edges gets much less of his attention. He also suggests giving the agent a long leash once, to learn where it breaks.

The archived May 2025 note shows the earlier version of the same habits. Back then Amp wrote an estimated 70–80% of his code. By the talk he says 99% of Amp is written by AI. Some advice fit the models of the time, such as short threads and a Playwright MCP server for screenshots. The framing has lasted: he called it paint-by-numbers, where he decides the architecture and constraints and the agent fills in the rest.

Own it end to end, and pair without a calendar

Ownership (July 2026) is an internal Slack message he sent the Amp team and later published almost unedited. When he asks someone to own something, he means from “we have a problem” until nobody needs to think about it again. That starts by separating the problem from a solution someone has already settled on, and runs through edge cases, failures, data, testing, deployment, telling colleagues and customers, and checking logs a week later. The reason is structural: a small team with no PMs and no QA department has nobody downstream to catch what the owner skipped.

What’s not okay is to implicitly assume that someone else will do the things here that you haven’t thought about.— Thorsten Ball, Ownership

He hedges it himself. Juniors should treat it as something to aspire to and ask for help along the way. Many items will not apply to a given change. He says he has only worked at startups and cannot speak for a company of 5,000.

The collaboration half comes from Zed. In The Lightness of Unscheduled Calls (February 2024), written four weeks into the job, he counts three or four scheduled pairing sessions against roughly 25 ad-hoc ones in 20 working days, held in Zed’s own shared channels. He credits the culture more than the technology: a reply of “wanna pair on it?” and no calendar invite.

At Amp both halves show up in the weekly rhythm he and Quinn describe in S2E6 (page 07 covers the episode in full). Most of the team has one fixed meeting a week, on Monday. He pre-fills the notes from his Sunday post, and after the discussion everyone gets a few minutes on a timer to write their goals for the week in silence before reading the headlines aloud. He asks anyone planning to ship to write the news post first, sometimes weeks early, because explaining the feature to a user tests whether the problem is clear. If teammates in the work-in-progress channel cannot follow the draft, the work needs to change. He compares it to README-driven development, and admits the stylistic back-and-forth over these posts can frustrate people.

What to steal.
  • Time how long your shell takes to start (time zsh -i -c exit, five runs). If it takes more than a couple of hundred milliseconds, profile your rc file and cut.
  • Before starting a feature, write the tasks and open questions in a todo.md. Re-read until a pass adds nothing, and show it to someone.
  • End the day mid-task, but only at a point where you know the next step.
  • Before sending a prompt, ask where the model will get the information. Point it at the files, paste the screenshot, name the constraint.
  • Ask for evidence before you read the diff: a screenshot, a demo, or a list of what was tested end to end. Save your close reading for the load-bearing code.
  • Write the announcement before you build. If a teammate cannot follow it, the problem is not clear yet.
