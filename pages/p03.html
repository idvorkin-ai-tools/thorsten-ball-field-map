The craft essays

The Register Spill posts that are not Joy & Curiosity issues, plus the posts he stars on his own blog, read as five arguments about how to do the job.
SourceRegister Spill, About page and its list of classic posts ↗
TL;DR — Register Spill started as one essay a week, written against a Sunday timer, before the Joy & Curiosity link issues took over. 83 posts in the archive are standalone essays rather than link roundups. Read together they make five claims. Milliseconds are felt, so speed is worth chasing everywhere, including your shell. The basics (test it by hand, read the error, know why the fix works) are the job, not a warm-up for it. Most hard engineering problems are people problems. Code has weight and an age, so write less of it, rewrite it without fear, and match your rigour to the project's phase. And he needs his work to count, which explains a good deal of his career.

The essays are short, usually under 1,200 words, and most start from something concrete: a slow shell, a TV scene, a colleague's question. The lessons are plain on purpose. In The Basics, his most-liked non-roundup post, he says they are called basics because they are fundamental, not because they are easy.

Speed you can feel

His clearest single argument is that latency adds up across things you do hundreds of times a day. How fast is your shell? (January 2024) asks you to time an interactive shell start and treat anything over a few hundred milliseconds as a defect. The reasoning is about frequency. You start shells, and redraw a prompt, more often than you run almost anything else, and every tool that hooks into your rc-file takes its own 10 or 20 ms. His fix is to profile first, then cut: fewer external commands at startup, a plainer prompt, less in general. He links the actual commits that took 150 ms and then 18 ms out of his own .zshrc.

Those who don’t honor the milliseconds will end up with seconds.— Thorsten Ball, How fast is your shell?

From 1s to 4ms (February 2024) is the same idea seen from inside an editor. After Zed was open-sourced, a Hacker News commenter said selecting every occurrence of a word took Zed about a second and Sublime Text about 200 ms. Thorsten and Antonio, a Zed co-founder, looked at the code. It called the "select next match" routine in a loop until there were no matches left, which is how most people would write it. Antonio's change was to inline that routine and do the work as one batch, which Thorsten compares to fixing an N+1 query in a web app. The new version still reads as high-level code, with no special data structures or unsafe tricks, and it found 2,351 matches in a 5,184-line file in 4 ms. Thorsten had spent his career on web backends, where a round trip to the nearest data centre takes longer than that, and the result changed his sense of what high-level code can cost.

Two essays stretch the argument past code. In the 2020 blog post No, typing can be the bottleneck he answers the usual retort to people who care about keyboards. Most of an engineer's output is prose (commit messages, reviews, tickets, design docs), and the cost of typing is not speed but effort. If typing is a chore you write less down, and the ideas stay in your head. Second's an Eternity walks through what drawing one line of text costs, 120 times a second, to show how much a computer does in the time we spend waiting.

Tools follow from this. In From Vim to Zed he explains leaving Vim after about 20 years. Zed's Vim mode was the entry condition. The draw was that it combines with features Vim lacks, especially multi-cursors and the multi-buffer, where search results from many files can be edited in one tab. He admits working at Zed is part of it, and adds a general rule: try new tools regularly, because you often bring something back.

The basics are the job

A second group of essays is about professional habits that separate good work from work that only looks finished. The Basics (March 2024) is a list, and most of its items push against one failure: handing off work you have not checked yourself. Test the change by hand even when automated tests pass. Prove each new test can fail by commenting out the code it covers. Know why your fix is a fix. Read the whole thing before you comment on it. The common thread is personal verification. Nobody else will catch what you skipped.

Polishing Forks gives the attitude behind the list. It starts from a scene in The Bear where a new hire in a top restaurant resents polishing forks, and Thorsten says he has given the head waiter's speech himself, about code formatting, commit messages and stray blank lines. He does not care much about typos in comments. He cares that people respect the chance to work somewhere good and do the small parts properly.

Several posts are about thinking before typing. Making a plan (June 2023) writes out a rough task list for a hypothetical avatar-upload feature. The list is incomplete on purpose. Writing it is what surfaces the open questions (what happens to the old avatar?) before day two instead of after, and it turns "I don't know" into a rough estimate. My favorite teacher comes at the same skill through school: a history teacher who made the class summarise everything in three sentences. Thorsten now uses that test on RFCs, on meetings, and on code. If a method needs eight sentences to describe, it probably does eight things.

Some are small tricks he presents as hard-won. Use data that looks like data says test values should never look like identifiers, so a log line that says user or 1 is never ambiguous; use my-test-user and 666. Leave something for tomorrow recommends stopping mid-task, but only when you know exactly what comes next, so the morning starts with typing rather than deciding.

How I use git (October 2024), one of his most-read posts, belongs here more than under tools. Its organising rule is that he optimises for what lands on the main branch, not for the commits on his branch. Each merged change should make sense alone, be revertable without dragging other work with it, and be bisectable. The rest follows: commit often as quicksaves, rebase freely until review, open draft PRs early so CI runs while he works, and split a two-line parser fix out of a UI change so either can be reverted alone. Messages should explain why. He ends by admitting his own merged PRs don't always follow these rules.

Engineering is a people problem

One of his first Register Spill posts, Two types of software engineers (April 2023), is still one of the most liked. Type 1 hears a problem and says it is easy, because people can just do X. Type 2 hears the same thing and says it is hard, for the same reason. He calls it a tiny theory and admits he can't draw the line precisely. He also rejects the reading that Type 2 is cynical. Shipping something people actually use is where the real work starts, and the mess is part of it.

I'd say that type 1 doesn't take people into account and type 2 knows that at the heart of all of their engineering work (and problems) are people.— Thorsten Ball, Two types of software engineers

The Most Powerful Law in Software (July 2023) applies the same idea to organisations. He used to shrug at Conway's Law, the observation that systems copy the communication structure of the teams that build them, because in small companies the product dictated the teams. Four years at Sourcegraph, as it grew from about twenty people to hundreds, changed his mind: parts of the product with a team were well built and parts without one went quiet. He reads the law as something you can use, not only suffer. Deciding the team structure of a new product shapes it as much as any wireframe.

Ownership (July 2026) is a Slack message to the Amp team, posted after a CTO shared it with their own staff. Owning something means taking it from "we have a problem" to "we don't have to think about it again". The checklist starts by asking whether the stated problem is really a solution in disguise and ends with confirming it works in production and telling the people who need to know. The reason is structural: a small team with no PMs and no QA department has nobody else to catch what you skip. He adds that juniors should aim for the list, not be expected to meet it, and that he has never worked at a company of thousands and can't say how it applies there.

Two short posts keep this from becoming an argument against technical skill. From Any Spot on the Field tells of a junior engineer who wrote excellent commit messages and wanted to move straight to strategy. Thorsten's answer was Zidane. A midfielder is valued for reading the game, but nobody would care if he couldn't also score from anywhere on the pitch. Be findable argues against the idea that good work will be discovered on its own. Showing your work is not bragging. It is making sure people can find it.

Code has mass, and an age

The fourth theme is how much code to write and how to treat it over time. Professional Programming: The First 10 Years, a starred favourite on his blog, states it most directly: code has mass, every unneeded line is ballast that must be read, tested and kept working, and that applies to tests too. The same post traces how his view of the future changed: early in his career "we will need this, so build it", later "we might need this, so prepare", now "build what we know we need". No, really: YAGNI names the result zombie code: it runs, it does nothing yet, and you pay to maintain it anyway.

Skin-Shedding Code (September 2024) describes the other half of keeping code light. At Zed, "going on a shred" means deleting something load-bearing and rebuilding it from the compiler errors instead of patching it. The team's GPUI rewrite before he joined ended as a +42,150 −330,105 pull request. He admits he doesn't know why the codebase survives this. He lists possible reasons (founders who know every file, deep domain experience, Rust, rewrites kept to a few files) without picking one. He does think the regular rewrites are healthy, because they burn off what nobody would add again.

The Age of a Software Project (May 2023) adds a time axis. An exploring project should learn fast and can skip best practices. A scaling one has to make its load-bearing parts actually load-bearing and wrap them in tests. A maintained one knows which files change weekly and which haven't been touched in years. He says the senior skill is knowing which phase you are in, and he complains that best practices usually arrive without that context. Glad I did it in Go shows the long end of the timeline: the 2016 code from Writing An Interpreter In Go, written for Go 1.7, still builds without warnings on Go 1.23, and readers have ported Monkey to around twenty languages without knowing Go.

Testing is where he has moved furthest. A Few Words on Testing (March 2024) opens with his credentials (more than half of all the code he has written is test code) and then a confession. Zed and Ghostty are among the best software he has used, and both have fewer tests than he expected, no slow UI suites, and PRs merged without tests. Maybe tests are a symptom of quality rather than its cause.

Maybe that’s all you need. That and people who give a damn.— Thorsten Ball, A Few Words on Testing

AI enters this theme through the question of which code deserves care. Surely not all code's worth it (December 2024) replies to readers who refuse AI because they want to learn, or because they love writing every line. He loves code too, but a 50-line script to convert his newsletter archive is write-only, has a small blast radius and teaches nothing. Letting a model write it leaves more time for the code that matters. By June 2026, Building Software Is Learning, another internal note to the Amp team, makes the general case. You cannot fully specify something new up front, because fully specifying it is what programming is. So "that's not what I meant" is unavoidable, and the only thing you control is how quickly you hear it: a one-hour prototype, a 30-minute spec, a fake demo video, the README example before the SDK. It is the age-of-a-project argument, compressed to a single feature.

Work that has to count

The most personal essay is With Nothing to Do (July 2024). About eight years earlier, his small startup spent weeks waiting to be acquired or go bankrupt, and the engineers had nothing that could change the outcome. Having nothing that mattered to do was miserable for him, and he wrote a note to himself that he still keeps.

I want my work to count. It should matter.— Thorsten Ball, With Nothing to Do

He draws concrete conclusions from it: he could not work in a research lab, he is wary of greenfield projects that might be scrapped regardless of what he does, and he prefers companies where engineering affects the outcome. It also explains why self-publishing his books suited him, because every hour he put in showed up in the result. Leaving Sourcegraph (December 2023) fits the pattern. After four and a half years he says he had drifted to the shallow end of the pool and wanted to learn again, and he left for Zed. Among the anecdotes is the lesson he says mattered most there: engineering has to serve the business, not itself.

Under the pragmatism there is real admiration for engineering. A Feat of Engineering is his favourite story to tell programmers: Apple's iOS 10.3 silently moved hundreds of millions of devices to a new file system, and almost nobody noticed. The essay is about the nerve it takes to press that button. How can you not be romantic about programming? (2020) finds the same awe in the volume and age of the code the world runs on. Curiosity is the other half. Learn more programming languages, even if you won't use them argues that each language is a lens on problems you keep after you put it down. They all use it (November 2024) turns that against programmers who tried an AI tool once and shrugged. Doubting the hype is fine with him. He doesn't understand a programmer who isn't even curious when programmers he admires are using it.

63 unpopular opinions

63 Unpopular Opinions (January 2024) is his. After freezing when a podcast host asked for one, he kept a phone note for six months and published it as a dump. He tells readers not to take it too seriously, except for the ones he "hid in there on purpose", and leaves you to work out which. Mixed in with pizza and charging cables, many of the items are the essays above in one line: taste is underrated and opinions overrated; there may be no link between how many tests a codebase has and product quality; delete any backlog item you can't do within three months; whether people enjoy working with you outweighs your skills, but you need both; programmers should switch ecosystems at least once or twice to become truly senior. That last one matches his own path from Ruby and JavaScript to Go and Rust.

The thorstenball.com favourites

His blog index stars seven posts. Four are discussed above: the first-ten-years retrospective, typing as the bottleneck, being romantic about programming, and learning more languages. The other three are about making things. What I didn't do to write a book (2017) says his first book took 11 months, with no productivity system at all, only the decision to keep going and a 5:45am alarm. The Tools I Use To Write Books (2018) describes plain Markdown files in git with the code kept in fenced blocks: clumsy, he admits, but it works exactly as he wants. Unicorn Unix Magic Tricks (2014), written up from a conference talk, takes apart the Ruby web server's master-and-worker processes to explain the Unix mechanisms behind what first looked like magic.
