Who Thorsten is, and what he's betting on

A philosophy student turned programmer, two books, two stints at Sourcegraph with a year at Zed in between, a co-founder seat at Amp, and a view of AI that went from wary to all-in within about two years.
Sourcethorstenball.com, his home page ↗
TL;DR — Thorsten Ball started programming for a salary in April 2012, wrote two self-published books on interpreters and compilers in Go, spent four and a half years at Sourcegraph, a year at Zed, then went back to Sourcegraph in January 2025 to build the coding agent Amp. In December 2025 Amp spun out as its own company and he became one of its twenty co-founders. By his own account he spent about two years treating AI as homework before a six-month turn changed his mind. He now bets that code itself is becoming cheap, that review and the craft of writing code by hand are fading, and that the scarce skill is knowing what to build and learning fast whether you built the right thing.

Thorsten's career runs through developer tools: language tooling, a text editor, then an agent. He likes to understand a machine down to the metal, and in 2025 that habit made him a convert to models he had earlier filed away as slot machines.

From philosophy to a paid internship

The most complete account of his early career is Professional Programming: The First 10 Years (May 2022). He built websites and IRC bots as a teenager, stopped, played guitar for a few years, and came back to programming in his twenties while he was studying philosophy. A paid internship with Robin settled the question of which career to pursue. That internship began in April 2012, which he counts as the start of his professional life.

The same essay lists what came next, without dates: junior developer for Robin, a developer at a small German startup (the link goes to flinc), a senior developer at a German startup inside a large German corporation, and then staff engineer at a fully remote US startup, which was Sourcegraph. His talks page confirms the flinc years: a lunch talk on Go at flinc in Darmstadt and a talk called "Software Engineering at Flinc", both in 2015.

The books came out of those German years. Writing An Interpreter In Go appeared in November 2016 and Writing A Compiler In Go in July 2018, both self-published (dates from his books page). Page 08 covers them. They matter here because they show the habit that runs through his whole career: take something that looks like magic, a programming language in this case, and show that it is ordinary code with tests.

Sourcegraph, the first time (2019 to 2023)

He joined Sourcegraph in April 2019. In Leaving Sourcegraph he traces the path there back to an essay by Steve Yegge that got him into compilers. Compilers led to language tooling, language tooling led to Sourcegraph, and at Sourcegraph he ended up reporting to Yegge.

That farewell post, written on his last day, 15 December 2023, is a list of anecdotes rather than a résumé, but the work shows through. He worked on Batch Changes, a tool for making the same change across many repositories, which went from his plea for one paying customer to a significant source of revenue. He also rewrote the internal developer tooling, co-hosted Dev Tool Time, and worked on the Cody assistant during what he calls the AI frenzy of 2023. The lesson he names as one of his biggest is that engineering has to serve the business, not itself.

His reason for leaving after four and a half years was about learning. He felt he had drifted into the shallow end and wanted to jump back into the deep end.

Zed (2024)

He started at Zed, to work on a new text editor, in January 2024 (New year, new job, new machine). Three months later he explained in From Vim to Zed why he had dropped Vim as his main editor after about twenty years. Zed's Vim mode was the requirement. The reasons were multi-cursors and the multi-buffer, which lets you edit excerpts from many files in one tab, and values he shared with the team: performance, simplicity, focus.

Zed was also where AI stopped being abstract for him. He worked on edit predictions and helped fine-tune a model, which a later section shows was the turning point. When he left, his summary in Joy & Curiosity #24 (26 January 2025) was nearly 500 merged pull requests, hundreds of pairing sessions, GPUs, async Rust and fine-tuned LLMs, and still the sense that something was missing.

Back to Sourcegraph, then Amp

The same issue announced the return to Sourcegraph. He says he talked to other companies first and chose this one because of where he thought programming was heading:

Now, I’m rejoining because I truly believe that programming is changing in fundamental ways.— Thorsten Ball, Joy & Curiosity #24, January 2025

Work on Amp started in February 2025. The podcast he co-hosts with Quinn Slack, Sourcegraph's CEO, Raising an Agent, published its first episode on 6 March 2025, before the product was public. His essay How to Build an Agent followed on 15 April, and Amp opened to everyone on 15 May, the date of his note Amp is now available. Here's how I use it.

The split from Sourcegraph is dated by Amp's own announcement of 2 December 2025. It says Amp was spinning out of Sourcegraph to become an independent research lab, that Amp's traction drove the decision and that Amp was profitable. Twenty people signed it as co-founders, Thorsten and Quinn among them. His own account in Joy & Curiosity #65 agrees: one of twenty co-founders. He adds that the idea moved over a few months from a maybe to something they had to do. Amp's about page now describes the company as an independent agent research lab that builds Amp and also runs Amp Labs, which places small teams of AI-native builders inside other companies.

In May 2026, describing a podcast appearance in Joy & Curiosity #87, he summed up the arc himself: from training to get better at Vim, through deciding that doesn't matter much anymore, to co-founding Amp.

The timeline

What ten years taught him

The 2022 essay is the best single summary of how he thought about software before agents. It is a loose list of opinions he had arrived at, several of them reversals of what he believed early on. The main threads are these.

  • Value to the customer is the goal. Type safety, test coverage, elegant APIs and fast tooling are means to that end. Refactoring a codebase that gets deleted four months later was wasted work, however good it was.
  • Code has mass. Every line has to be read, tested and kept working, so less code is better, and that includes tests.
  • Feedback loops are never wasted effort. Ideally a test run is three keystrokes and under a second.
  • Code reviews are imperfect. Other people miss bugs in review too, so review is one way to verify code among several, and not every change deserves a deep one.
  • Negativity spreads. Cynicism is easy to mistake for engineering rigour. He decided to do the harder thing and look for what could be good in an idea.

Read in 2026, the list looks like groundwork for what came later. Value to the customer as the only goal turns, four years on, into his doubt that good code will matter at all. Imperfect reviews turn into his claim that code review is dying. The feedback-loop point becomes his 2026 note to the Amp team. His ideas about software didn't flip when agents arrived. Agents removed the cost of typing, and the priorities he already held were left standing on their own.

The values on his home page fit the same picture. The current page source still holds a "What I value" list, commented out so it no longer renders. Its last line reads "Curiosity, joy, beauty, improvements, change", and two of those words became the name of his newsletter series (page 02).

From wary to all-in, with dates

The usual story is a skeptic who converted. The record is more mixed, because what he wrote at the time and what he wrote looking back don't quite agree.

2023: open, not excited. In The Messy Page (May 2023) he says he doesn't much mind buggy code from an AI assistant, because a messy draft beats an empty file. None of this reads as hostile.

March 2024: one tool among many. How I Use AI logs two working days of ChatGPT, Zed's inline assist and Copilot. Some answers help, some are wrong, and whole afternoons pass without any AI at all. He says he has no strong opinion on whether AI is good or bad.

November 2024: curiosity as a duty. They all use it grows out of a hallway conversation with an engineer who had tried AI once and given up. Thorsten lists programmers he admires who use it and argues that curiosity is supposed to be what programmers share. He is still careful to say he isn't asking anyone to kneel at the AGI altar.

January 2025: convinced. Days after he left Zed, How might AI change programming? opens with:

AI will change programming. I’m convinced of it now.— Thorsten Ball, How might AI change programming?, January 2025

The argument is modest: even if progress stopped that day, making a certain kind of code cheap would change programming the way compilers and version control did. The rest of the post is open questions. Will new languages struggle without training data? Will a context file become the new robots.txt? Will code get more verbose so that models can read it?

May 2025: the retrospective. There's Beauty in AI carries the subtitle "From skepticism and dread to fascination and amazement", and it is the source for the conversion story. He says that for two years AI felt like homework he had to do to keep up, and that LLMs seemed like slot machines: you pull the lever and sometimes win. Two things at Zed changed that. Cursor's tab completion predicted his next edit and where it would be, faster than his twenty years of Vim. Then fine-tuning a model showed him how data shapes its behaviour, and the slot machine turned out to have wiring he could reason about.

Then, throughout the last six months, I changed my view on LLMs completely.— Thorsten Ball, There’s Beauty in AI, May 2025

So, in fairness to both records: in 2023 and 2024 he used AI regularly and defended trying it, but he didn't love it. The skepticism he describes was about fascination more than usefulness. He couldn't see the mystery in it that had drawn him to compilers. The change came when the black box opened, which is the same thing his books do for interpreters.

The bet

From late 2025 his claims grow stronger, and the hedges change.

A New Kind of Code (September 2025) asks you to stop judging agents by whether they write code the way you do. Agents are a button that produces 500 lines of working, mostly dumb code in a minute. Code used to be bolts and screws, expensive and precise. Now there is glue as well, and the useful question is what you can build with glue. When Amp spun out three months later, he wrote:

Today I’m more convinced than ever that what we’re going through is only in its opening movement.— Thorsten Ball, Joy & Curiosity #65, December 2025

In Joy & Curiosity #79 (March 2026) he responds to a post arguing that each layer of review makes a team ten times slower. In a parenthetical aside he says he doesn't think model-written code will need close-up review in the near future. Building Software Is Learning (June 2026), an internal note to the Amp team that he later published, makes the positive case. When you build something new you can't fully specify it up front, because specifying it fully is the programming. So the job is to shorten the time between "let me try something" and finding out you were wrong, using prototypes, specs, fake demos, draft announcements and daily shipping.

The fullest statement is What I believe about the future of software development (19 September 2026). He first posted it on X, then moved it to his blog to put on record what he believed that month. Grouped by theme, the claims are:

  • Verification moves up a level. Code review is effectively dead. Humans will review systems and how they fit together, not lines in pull requests. Unit tests may go too.
  • The craft splits in two. Writing code by hand goes the way of the Italian shoemaker. Building software, meaning knowing which problem to solve, how to ship and how to get feedback, matters more than ever. Most bugs will be cases of asking for the wrong thing.
  • Old tools and habits wash out. The terminal is dead for humans, and he says this as someone who loves the terminal. So are the PM/design/engineering triad and the "meat proxy" engineer who passes tickets to agents. "Good code" was mostly about what is cheap for humans to work with, and that may stop mattering.
  • Tokens are the new computing paradigm. Smarter models beat cheap ones because they make fewer mistakes. People without access to enough tokens will be priced out for a while. Models will get fast enough to generate interfaces on the fly.

The hedges are part of the bet. He says some items are only observations of what is already true at companies like Amp, that the shift will take a generation, and that people will still be paid to write code in ten years. The question he leaves you with is whether you want that job. Performance-critical work by humans survives, but as an edge case.

Amp states the same bet at company level. The about page quotes the closing commitments of Amp's manifesto: build for a world where intelligence is abundant, continuous and cheap; don't keep old software processes out of habit; don't wait for the end state to become obvious; go to the frontier first. These are Amp's words rather than his, but they match his essays.
